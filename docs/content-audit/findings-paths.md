# Paths and chooser audit (task P1)

Method: loaded DATA through `src/manifest.js` (script in `scratchpad/work/paths/`), dumped all 21 paths (100 stages, about 690 steps), measured linked-topic and game-lens length at 200 words per minute against each step's minutes, counted cross-path overlap, and enumerated `choosePath` for all 81 goal, level and time combinations. Every stage and every recall answer outline was read; the answer outlines were judged for internal consistency and against the linked topic's claims, not re-sourced from outside. Read-only; no repo edits.

Counts: 6 high, 15 medium, 11 low (32 findings).

Cross-cutting facts:
- Read time is small (most topic steps are 1 to 6 minutes of text); step minutes are mostly task time. Only 37 of 117 game steps have a read time above 70% of their minutes, so time problems are local, not systemic.
- 39 of 100 stage `build` lines are "export the tool's result or checklist result"; all five stages of live-game-backend, netcode and build-and-release are like this.
- About 12 of 400 recall questions are personal or unanswerable from an outline ("in your case", "what did X show you").
- Cross-path overlap is low. The only large one is Godot vs Unity (13 shared steps, by design) and Godot vs game-AI (8). No path is redundant.

## 1. Verdict per path

1. **game-designer-foundations** (beginner, 9.75h). Sound. Outcome is checkable, order is clean (player, fantasy, loop, decisions, feedback, hypothesis) and every stage has a tool. Weak spots: S3 and S4 are labelled intermediate for a beginner path; two recall answers (which of the nine questions "kills the most ideas in your experience") are personal; it assumes the learner already has a game idea with no step that produces one (idea-to-prototype does that, but the chooser picks foundations first).
2. **systems-designer** (intermediate, 12.5h). Good coverage: relationships, economy, progression, content vs depth, smells to experiments, audit. Weaknesses: S3 repeats the foundations rule audit and depth topic with the same task; S5 says "your redesigned system" but S4 only writes a hypothesis, so no redesign exists; two smell steps in S2 both ask "your last three unlocks".
3. **level-and-ux-designer** (intermediate, 12h). Coherent and practical (pacing graph, feedback matrix, silent onboarding audit, dissection). S3 repeats the feedback matrix task from foundations S3. S5 goal says "take a real level from another game apart" but only one of six steps does. Recall in S2 and S5 asks "in your case" questions.
4. **games-that-broke-the-mould** (intermediate, 13h). Good selection and the ordering (teach, knowledge, time, hybrids, presentation) is sensible. Mostly reading and lens summaries; ten game steps are budgeted 15 to 25 minutes for whole-page reads that take 18 to 22 minutes before the task, so the path is nearer 14.5 to 15h. Outcome ("through ten lenses") is not checkable. Recall is mostly game trivia (Worms retreat, Press Turn half icon).
5. **study-the-hits-play** (intermediate, 12h). The dimensions/mastery/content/emergence/rhythm arc works. But audience says "designers who know the fundamentals" and prereq is empty, while its sibling games-that-broke-the-mould requires foundations. Steps assume "your game" exists. Recall is close to game trivia (Tetris seven-bag, Dota last-hit).
6. **idea-to-prototype-30-days** (beginner, 11.25h). Best motivation and the right shape, but the outcome ("a tested core loop... playtest behind it") is not delivered: no step builds a prototype, the S2 engine steps are 15 minutes of "build a room and export to the web" (GameMaker) and "write a scene and run Lint" (Ren'Py), and the S3 build asks the learner to run a playtest of something that does not exist. S4 leans on a Go backend case study for a design beginner.
7. **study-the-hits-worlds** (intermediate, 11.25h). Solid for premise, choice consequence, story timeline, audiovisual grammar, positioning. Same prereq gap as study-the-hits-play. Recall is almost entirely lens trivia (Viewfinder's five hubs, FFXII turning point, ETS2 radio). "Fifteen well-known games" includes Viewfinder and Clair Obscur.
8. **technical-lead** (advanced, 10.75h). Content is good (delegation, review, conventions, incidents, scope, process) and practice is concrete. Problem is the prereq: systems-designer and level-and-ux-designer (24.5h of design) for an audience of "senior designers or engineers", which blocks engineers and drives the chooser. Coverage gaps: giving feedback and performance conversations, conflict, upward/stakeholder communication, career growth of others. The design-review checklist is run three times in five stages.
9. **interview-prep-designer** (intermediate, 11.75h). Well built: rehearse out loud, STAR stories, real engineering constraints from case-study parts, fun diagnostic, pillars. Prereqs (systems and level-ux, 24.5h) are heavy for someone who only needs interview prep and has skip questions. Missing: design test/take-home, portfolio walk-through, design-doc critique, partner mock (all rehearsal is solo). Some recall is generic ("what word did you catch yourself using").
10. **casual-game-people-keep** (intermediate, 10.25h). Strong specificity (soft-launch gates, playable-ad caps, remote config, ethics). Outcome says "prove retention and install cost", which a 10-hour plan cannot do; the path only plans. S1's build ("build or paper-prototype a level a stranger can play") has no building step behind it. Only Google Play is covered as a platform; iOS is absent from S4 soft launch.
11. **game-skills-elsewhere** (intermediate, 8h). Distinctive and useful (skill inventory, destination, CV, interviews). Chooser sends every "New to it" learner here although the audience is experienced developers; `next` points to interview-prep-engineer/designer, whose prereqs are 25 to 45h. The farmer answer topic and its USDA recall question are a long detour for the outcome.
12. **gameplay-engineer-godot** (beginner, 14.25h). Content is right (loop, failure, feel, AI, prototype/build health) and each engine step has runnable API-level tasks. Problems: prereq is design foundations, whose overview topics it then repeats (core-loop, decisions, mechanics, feedback, juice, controls); engine orientation comes after code (S1 step 6 after steps 2 and 5); no time for install and project setup; S1 is 2D but S4 is 3D; game steps ask for builds that do not exist (FNAF test of a power drain, 15 min).
13. **gameplay-engineer-unity** (beginner, 15h). Mirror of Godot with the same issues. Extras: Deus Ex step asks for "one automated test per route for a similar door in your scene" (no such door exists, 20 min); source control appears in S5 here but not in Godot.
14. **live-game-backend-engineer** (intermediate, 15h). Strong, concrete backend content tied to one real Go case study. Issues: prereq is game-designer-foundations for an audience of working backend engineers; Go is assumed throughout though the outcome does not say so; stage ids are out of order (s1, s2, s5, s3, s4); every build is a tool export (delegation planner, AI-verify checklist), so no build proves the goal; tool steps (delegation planner used as a "design review") are forced by the one-tool-per-stage rule.
15. **netcode-server-engineer** (advanced, 14.75h). Good on authority, determinism, wire format, matchmaking, fan-out, anti-cheat. Big gap: no topic or step on client-side prediction, reconciliation, interpolation, lag compensation or rollback (there is no topic for them in the library at all), although the audience says "latency, authority, and reconciliation". S5 spends four steps on a Unity port's obfuscation and reconnect quirks. Smash Bros step is 10 minutes to "play or watch" online and local matches. System map is the tool in all five stages and every build is a map export.
16. **build-and-release-engineer** (intermediate, 14.5h). Rich real-pipeline material and useful "count how many of yours" tasks. Weaknesses: prereq is design foundations; outcome says "build a player from a single parameterised entry point" but no step builds one (steps compare the learner's pipeline with a Unity case study); five different engine guides (Blender, Unreal, web, Cocos, Defold) in 12 to 18 minutes each with commands to run; all five builds are exports of maps or checklists.
17. **game-ai-programmer** (intermediate, 9.9h). Best-scoped engineering path (job and tells, senses and memory, director/ally, budget and fallback). But prereq lists both gameplay-engineer-godot and gameplay-engineer-unity (29h) though the audience is "Godot or Unity"; S1 is labelled beginner; the Halo step asks for an arena with leader and three followers in 25 minutes including reading.
18. **interview-prep-engineer** (intermediate, 9.75h). The concept (specific answers with real trade-offs) is good, but the path prereqs are godot, live-game-backend and netcode (about 44h across three specialisations) and content is backend-heavy (layering, authority, monitoring). There is no algorithm, data-structure, language (C++/C#) or gameplay-math practice, no system-design walkthrough and no coding exercise; every build is a 60-second spoken answer. The Fortnite/UEFN platform step and the Beat Saber test-plan step do not serve a general engineering interview.
19. **studio-practice-ai-era** (advanced, 13h). Coherent for leads working with AI (ownership split, failure modes, review rules, model choice, scope). It doubles as a general producer path (S5 documents/engine/agile, S6 PC store checklist), so the name overpromises. Everything is "your team", so it cannot be started without a team.
20. **ship-it** (intermediate, 10h). Good calendar/access/ratings/patch spine and honest builds (a suspend/resume test log). Drifts from the title: Roblox/UGC, Quest, CrazyGames, soft-launch playable ads; and S2 asks producers to sketch a CI job. Chooser sends "New to it" learners who have no game to ship.
21. **ai-engineering-for-game-devs** (intermediate, 12.5h). Good arc from tokens to verification to in-game boundary to store disclosure. Practice is notes and checklists; no step makes a model call from game code, measures latency or cost, or validates structured output, which the S4 build ("design one AI feature") assumes. S4 forces `engine:godot` on all learners.
