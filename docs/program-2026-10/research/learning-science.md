# Learning science evidence review for the Playable curriculum and the "mini-adventure" idea

Date: 2026-10-05. Scope: design rules for a free, self-paced website for adults (game design and game engineering). Read-only research; nothing in the repo was changed.

## 0. How to read this (evidence quality, stated plainly)

- **I read no paper in full.** WebFetch was blocked or returned binaries for most publishers (Springer, Sage, PubMed, the Würzburg PDF). Every number below comes from search-result text: a publisher abstract, a repository abstract, or a secondary page. The status column says which.
  - **Abstract** = the abstract or a repository abstract, seen in search results. Numbers are the authors' own.
  - **Secondary** = the number came through a blog, press release or encyclopedia page. Treat as "probably right, recheck before quoting in public".
  - **Not verified** = I know the claim from memory only, or the search did not show it. These are written as such and are not used as a basis for a rule.
- Effect sizes are standardised mean differences (d or Hedges g). Rough convention: 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large. Most come from school or undergraduate samples and lab or classroom conditions. **None are from adults self-studying a game-development site.** Every rule is therefore an inference. Verify with this site's own data (review-queue answers, checkpoint completion) before calling any rule proven.
- Contested findings are marked **CONTESTED**.

**Reading across:** the average gamification effect is real but small and fragile on motivation. The cognitive gain is the most robust piece. The big risks documented in this evidence are extrinsic rewards (Deci), decorative additions (Rey), novelty fading (Hamari), and streaks (no learning evidence). The big supports are intrinsic integration (Habgood), informational feedback (Deci, Wisniewski), and visible progress (weak, by analogy).

### 2.2 Verdict on the owner's idea

**Verdict: partly supported. Keep the mechanics, thin the fiction, and make sure nothing animated or locked sits between the learner and the content.**

What the evidence **supports**:

1. A checkpoint recast as an encounter is the same free-recall test (items 1-3) with feedback (item 12), so the learning gain is intact as long as the retrieval still happens before the reveal.
2. "The boss is the lesson check" is an instance of intrinsic integration (Habgood & Ainsworth). The check is the mechanic. It is not a quiz attached to a game.
3. A map that shows where you are and what is next is competence feedback and low-cost orientation. The goal-gradient literature is an argument that people respond to visible distance remaining, but its evidence is about reward programs.
4. Thin game fiction was associated with better behavioural outcomes in Sailer & Homner (not cognitive).

What the evidence **warns against**:

1. **Tangible or expected rewards** (stars, loot, unlock chests, XP totals) undermine intrinsic motivation in Deci et al. The site already chose no streaks and no badges. Keep that. A "boss defeated" screen can be informational (what you recalled and what to review) without being a reward.
2. **Decorative animation or story beats between reading steps.** Seductive-detail evidence shows harm to retention and transfer, and the walking sprite is exactly that if it runs while someone is trying to read.
3. **Slowing experts down.** Expertise reversal (item 8): guidance and obstacles that help novices hurt experienced learners. An overworld that forces walking to the next step costs an expert minutes per stage.
4. **Competition.** Sailer & Homner did not show competition alone as a clear win, and this is a solo site anyway. No leaderboard, no ranking.
5. **Novelty.** Hamari et al. flag novelty effects. A story that excited the learner on day 1 may be noise by day 30. Do not design for the first session.
6. **Fiction that leaks into pedagogy.** "Defeat" and "HP" language can imply failure when the learner misses an answer. Missing a recall item is the learning event (retrieval with feedback), so the framing must make "missed" a normal, neutral state.
7. **Self-rating as damage.** If rating "got it" makes the boss lose HP, learners have an incentive to rate generously, and self-judgements are noisy anyway (items 18-19).

### 2.3 Is something better? A concrete improved design

I did not find evidence that an adventure beats a plain, well-built progress map with spaced review. The strongest, cheapest improvement is to make **progress mean mastery by recall** instead of "I clicked it". The adventure is a skin on top of that.

**Overworld (the path page, with a "plain list" toggle)**
- Shows the path as a simple map with the stages as regions, the current region highlighted, and a clear "next step" marker.
- Each region shows three real facts: steps read, checkpoint state (not tried / some recalled / mostly recalled), and how many Review items are due. The state comes from stored self-ratings. It is not a click count.
- The little player marker may move between regions with a short transition (under about one second). It never moves on a step page. `prefers-reduced-motion` turns it into a jump.
- Every region is open at all times. The map is a **view**, not a gate. A learner can open any step in any region at any time, including by the plain list.
- No points, no totals, no streak, no badge, no "unlocked" language. Use "you've recalled 4 of 6" and "3 to review today".

**Castle = a stage checkpoint.** Entering the castle means opening the stage's checkpoint page. It has the same content as today (questions, outlines, build task), nothing hidden, and a visible "enter later" link.

**Boss = the stage's set of free-recall questions, presented one at a time.** Each question is a "phase" of the boss. The boss is "down" when every question has been recalled and the learner is happy with the outline comparison. There is no health bar draining from the learner and no lose condition.

**How a fight works (free recall plus self-rating)**
1. The question appears alone. The learner writes or says an answer from memory. The step text is not on screen. (Retrieval, items 1-3.)
2. Before the reveal, one tap on "How sure am I?" with three levels. This is a judgement made before the answer is visible, which is what keeps it honest (items 18-19). It is used only for scheduling.
3. Reveal the answer outline as a short list of ideas a good answer should contain, plus a note on the common slip (informational feedback, item 12).
4. The learner ticks which outline ideas they had. Three outcomes: missed, partial, got it. Using the tick list instead of a plain "did I get it?" button makes the rating more objective than a gut call.
5. "Missed" and "partial" questions are marked for the Review queue (1 day) and re-asked once at the end of the fight (a second retrieval, with the same feedback). "Got it" goes to the queue at the right step of 1,2,4,8,16.
6. End screen: what you recalled, what is coming back and when, and a link to the exact steps behind the missed items. That is feedback with an action. No score, no stars, no "boss defeated" bonus.

**What must never block reading**
- No locked steps, no locked castles, no "defeat the boss to continue". Mastery learning (item 11) supports requiring correction, but a free site for adults does not need a hard gate to get the benefit. This one is my judgement, not an evidence finding.
- No animation, sprite or story text on topic pages, step pages or inside the question pane while answering.
- No full-screen transitions that must finish. All transitions are skippable and removable by a setting.
- "I already know this" becomes the **test-out**: the learner can face the stage's boss first as a pre-test. If they recall most items, the stage is marked as known and the review items are queued. If they do not, they go to the first step. This helps experts (item 8) and adds a retrieval event for everyone.
- Keyboard and screen reader parity: the map must have a text equivalent, and the fight must work with plain form controls.

**Cheap staging.** Phase 1: the map with real mastery states and the plain list, no sprite. Phase 2: the boss presentation of the existing checkpoint. Phase 3 (only if learners use it): the sprite and story skin. Measure each phase against the plain version. If the plain checkpoint gets the same completion and review rates, the skin is not earning its cost.

**Rules for this site (derived from the above; where the evidence is thin I say so)**

1. **Default is a static diagram** with labels next to the parts they name and a caption stating the one idea (signalling, coherence). Tversky et al. and Berney's small overall effect say animation is not a free upgrade.
2. **Use animation only when change over time is the idea**: a game loop, a state machine firing, physics integration, input buffering, a camera follow, easing curves. In that case use a stepped diagram (previous/next buttons, each step labelled, a final frame that stays) rather than a looping autoplay (segmenting). Pacing is contested; stepping with a "play all" option covers both readings.
3. **Make "play" something the learner chooses.** No autoplay, no looping, honour `prefers-reduced-motion`, and always show the final state as a static image with a text description.
4. **If it moves but does not teach, it is a seductive detail.** Decorative motion (the sprite, ambient particles, scroll effects on content pages) is where the harm evidence points. Keep it out of the reading column.
5. **Prefer an interactive model to a movie when the learner can change inputs** (a tweakable jump arc, a spawn-rate slider). That is the explorable-explanations idea. It is a practitioner heuristic here, so build one and watch whether learners use it before building many.
6. **Video** is for things that are procedural and screen-based: "where do I click in the editor", "how this looks at 60 fps", motion and game feel, debugging walkthroughs. Evidence points to the procedural-motor and realistic cases (Höffler & Leutner).
   - Keep each video short, one idea each, and split anything longer into chapters (Guo et al.; segmenting). A hard cap of about 6 minutes is a working target, not a finding.
   - Narrate it, with no duplicate on-screen paragraphs (Berney's auditory-commentary and no-text findings; coherence/redundancy are not verified here).
   - Always publish the same steps as text beside it. Text is searchable, skimmable and keeps the video optional. Do not make a video the only source for a step.
   - Show a hand-drawn or live-screen view, not a recorded lecture (Guo et al.). Informal narration beats polished.
7. **Where text beats video:** concepts, definitions, trade-offs, checklists. Experts skim text faster than they can watch.
8. **Every diagram and video is checked against the question "which checkpoint question does this help answer?"** If none, cut it.

## 4. Set aside (considered and rejected or deferred)

- **Learning styles / "visual learner" quiz**: rejected; no support (Pashler et al. 2008).
- **Streaks, daily-goal nagging, loss-aversion messaging**: rejected; no peer-reviewed learning evidence found, motivational risk (Deci, "streak creep"), and already a stated site decision.
- **XP, badges, loot, unlockable cosmetics**: rejected; tangible/expected rewards undermine intrinsic motivation (Deci et al. 1999).
- **Leaderboards and competition**: rejected; solo site, and Sailer & Homner did not show competition alone as a win.
- **Hard gating of steps behind the boss**: rejected; no evidence that blocking is required for the benefit, and it blocks experts (expertise reversal). Judgement call, not an evidence finding.
- **Mandatory "try first" productive-failure pages**: rejected as mandatory; offered as optional. Evidence is mostly school and university maths and science and is contested against guided-instruction arguments.
- **Replacing free recall with multiple choice**: rejected; recall tests gave larger benefits than recognition in Rowland, though Adesope's summary says MC did well (CONTESTED); production matches the real task.
- **Ego depletion**: not used as a design rule; the effect has failed to replicate in large multi-lab work (known from memory, not verified here).
- **Calibration or "confidence score" shown to the learner**: rejected; Dunning-Kruger is contested and self-ratings are noisy.
- **Highlighting, rereading, summarisation features**: rejected; low utility (Dunlosky et al.).
- **Bloom's "2 sigma" as a promise**: not used; it combined tutoring and mastery and is not a result for a website.
- **Deliberate-practice marketing claims**: rejected; Macnamara et al. show a modest variance share outside music and games, and the claim is contested.
- **Goal-gradient as proof that a map increases learning**: rejected as proof; the evidence is from loyalty cards and gift certificates. Used only as support for showing progress.
- **Endowed-progress ("start with a free stamp"), pre-testing effect, hypercorrection effect, spacing with expanding intervals vs equal**: considered; I did not retrieve a source for them in this session, so no rules rest on them. Worth a second research pass.
- **Self-determination theory as a source of design rules**: I did not retrieve a primary SDT source; its content here is used only via the Deci et al. result.
- **Mayer's coherence, redundancy, pre-training principles with effect sizes**: considered; no numbers retrieved. The rules above use the principles qualitatively and say so.
- **Autoplay explainer videos, long lecture-style videos, talking-head-only videos**: rejected on Guo et al. and the segmenting evidence.
- **Cognitive load theory as a stand-alone source**: used only through worked examples, expertise reversal and segmenting; I did not find a quantitative CLT meta-analysis in this pass.
- **Social features** (study groups, shared progress): out of scope for a free self-paced site; Sailer & Homner's social findings are about competition with collaboration.

## 5. Judgment calls and limits of this review

- Used search-result abstracts as "Abstract" and labelled secondary pages as such. A cautious reader should open the DOI before quoting any number externally.
- Where the search tool returned a number for the wrong paper (the productive-failure effect sizes in the results belonged to a different, flipped-learning analysis), I discarded it and left Sinha & Kapur's g as not verified.
- Wikipedia and blog pages are cited only where nothing better was returned, and are marked Secondary.
- No wording in this file was taken from the repository; the "Confirmed" site facts (checkpoints, 1,2,4,8,16 review, no streaks or badges, "I already know this") were supplied in the brief and not re-checked against the code.
